# What proven review UIs demand of Ascribe

Ascribe review's core layout (margin threads at wide widths, markers opening a panel at narrow widths, detached threads listed at the top, resolved threads collapsed, a three-way changes/final/original toggle) matches established convention in both document tools and code-review tools, and it fills the two gaps GitHub documents in its own rendered diff: no commenting in the rendered view and failure on complex changes. The evidence points to four places where the design carries known, documented risk. First, the word "Pending" is the most complained-about label in GitHub's review flow, with a 423-upvote thread from people who left reviews unsent for days or months. Second, every batching tool now previews the pending comments at submit time and GitHub hides Discard inside the submit surface, so a bar with a count, a summary box and a sibling Discard button sits behind the baseline. Third, a non-movable bottom overlay is the exact case WCAG 2.4.11 names, and Astro's stock toolbar window covers most of a small viewport. Fourth, colour and `ins`/`del` semantics are both unreliable channels, so visible text labels and a navigable change list have to carry the meaning. Two design choices have no current precedent in either direction: the connector line (no surveyed product ships one, and Word removed its own) and collapsing removed blocks to an expandable stub. The research is strong on vendor-documented behaviour and W3C criteria and weak on controlled studies; no usability research was found on margin versus inline threads for review, prose diff granularity, move presentation, or how much deleted text to show.

A note on how to read this report. Sourced claims carry an inline link. Statements introduced as "inference" are the researchers' own reasoning applied to Ascribe and are not stated by any source. Sources marked "(snippet)" were seen only as search-result excerpts or issue titles and deserve verification before being quoted. Quoted strings were read through a summarising fetch tool, so treat them as near-verbatim.

## Margin threads match convention, but the connector line and the orphan list need care

### Highlight plus proximity is the linking device every document tool uses

Every document tool surveyed places a highlight on the anchor and a card in a right-hand margin or panel, and pairs that contextual view with an exhaustive, filterable list. Word's contextual view puts comments "in the Comments pane on the right margin near the insertion point", with a separate list view of "all active and resolved comments" ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/using-modern-comments-in-word)). Google Docs offers hidden, minimised (icons that preview on hover) and expanded densities plus a "Show all comments" panel filterable by open versus resolved ([Google Docs Help](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/65129)). Notion has a Default mode with comments at the right and a Minimal mode showing only icons that open on click ([Notion Help](https://www.notion.com/help/comments-mentions-and-reminders)). Figma consolidates closely pinned comments into zoom-dependent clusters and adds a searchable, sortable sidebar ([Figma Help](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/360041547593-View-and-manage-comments)).

**Word explicitly removed connector lines**: "we've made anchoring the comment to the content easier to understand and we no longer use the dotted lines you're familiar with from classic comments," relying instead on anchor highlights and reciprocal emphasis when either end is selected ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/using-modern-comments-in-word)). Inference: Ascribe's connector line departs from current practice. No source shows it to be wrong, and there is a real argument for it. A Google patent describes the problem that limited margin space leaves annotations "positioned far from the portions of the document that they reference" and proposes merging conflicting annotations into an expandable stack ([US10042832](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10042832)); a patent describes a design, not shipped behaviour. The inference for the design review is that the line earns its place only when stacking displaces a thread away from its block, that reciprocal hover and focus highlighting should exist regardless, and that the review should test what happens when many threads cluster on adjacent blocks. The two overflow strategies with precedent are stack-and-expand (the patent, Figma clusters) and degrading the column to icons (Google minimised, Notion Minimal).

Code-review tools take the opposite placement, inline under the commented line, and then spend effort mitigating its cost. GitHub's new Files changed experience shows code comments "minimized by default" and adds a side panel to "discover comments, including the ability to search for text" ([GitHub Changelog, June 2025](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-06-26-improved-pull-request-files-changed-experience-now-in-public-preview/)); Gerrit's Comments tab shows each thread with "the snippet of code (where the comment was made) as context" ([Gerrit 3.4](https://www.gerritcodereview.com/3.4.html)); Reviewable groups discussions as To reply, Unresolved and Resolved with a counter ([Reviewable](https://docs.reviewable.io/discussions)). Inference: a margin column avoids the reading interruption these mitigations exist to fix, so Ascribe's choice suits rendered prose, but the universal second surface (a list of all threads with state filters and context) is not optional. A small 2025 study of AI margin notes reported a preference for margin notes over content inserted into the document flow ([arXiv 2509.09840](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09840v3), snippet; sample size unchecked). No controlled study compares margin and inline threads for code or documentation review.

### Hiding orphans reads as data loss; silently re-placing them reads as wrong

Tools handle a lost anchor in three ways, and each has documented consequences.

| Strategy | Examples | Documented outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Detach and label | GitHub "Outdated", Hypothesis Orphans tab, Google Docs "Original content deleted" (snippet), Figma unattached comments (snippet) | Users lose sight of the thread next to the replacement content |
| Re-port to a best guess, marked as approximate | Gerrit ported comments, Phabricator ghosts, Reviewable dog-ear | Vendors warn the position can be wrong |
| Silently resolve | Confluence on cut-and-paste (snippet) | Anchor loss is conflated with resolution |

Hypothesis is the clearest evidence for the first rule: before 2017 it hid annotations that failed to anchor, users returning to a page concluded their annotations were missing, and the fix was a dedicated Orphans tab shown only when orphans exist ([Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is/blog/showing-orphaned-annotations/)). Ascribe's top-of-page detached list follows that fix. GitHub's rationale for detaching is that "automatic tools have no way of knowing if the comment targeted at the old line still makes sense on the new line", and the cost is a long-running complaint that verifying a fix takes two tabs because "the fixed code isn't shown there"; a September 2025 user report says the new experience shows outdated comments "at the top instead of inline" ([GitHub community discussion #23138](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/23138)).

The tools that re-port treat the approximate position as a distinct, visibly caveated state. Phabricator's ported comments "have a pale, ghostly appearance and include a button which allows you to jump back to the original context", with the warning that "it is not possible to always port comments to the same lines humans would" ([Phabricator](https://secure.phabricator.com/book/phabricator/article/differential_inlines/)). Reviewable shows a red dog-ear when "the current context may be inaccurate because of significant changes" ([Reviewable](https://docs.reviewable.io/files)). Gerrit's API warns that porting can drop the range, convert to a file comment or move files, and that callers "shouldn't rely on the exact logic" ([Gerrit REST API](https://gerrit-review.googlesource.com/Documentation/rest-api-changes.html), snippet). GitLab adds a system note linking to the version comparison that made the thread outdated ([GitLab issue #24468](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/24468), snippet). Figma users ask for a label such as "attached content deleted" because it is hard to tell what an orphan referred to ([Figma forum](https://forum.figma.com/suggest-a-feature-11/make-it-possible-to-reattach-unattached-comments-33090), snippet).

Inference for Ascribe: "outdated" (the block exists but its content changed) and "detached" (no block found) are different states and deserve different treatments. A detached entry should say why it is detached, show the original context (the quoted source lines or diff hunk GitHub already supplies), and link to the nearest surviving content where one exists. An outdated thread kept beside its block needs a visual caveat and a way back to the original text. On the anchoring mechanism itself, the standards position is redundancy: Hypothesis stores range, position and text-quote selectors (exact text plus 32 characters of prefix and suffix) and tries them from most exact to fuzziest ([Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is/blog/fuzzy-anchoring/), a 2013 description), and the W3C Web Annotation model treats multiple selectors as alternatives for one selection ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/annotation-model/), snippet). The inference is that Ascribe should classify each thread as exact, approximately re-anchored, or orphaned and render the three differently. The often-repeated finding that users prefer an orphan to a misplaced annotation is attributed to Brush et al. ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/robust-annotation-positioning-in-digital-documents/)), but only the abstract was read, so that specific claim is unverified.

### Collapsing resolved threads follows code review; hiding them follows documents

GitHub collapses a resolved conversation and marks it resolved, because "this makes it easier to find conversations that still need to be addressed" ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/collaborating-with-pull-requests/reviewing-changes-in-pull-requests/commenting-on-a-pull-request)); GitLab's "resolved threads are collapsed, but users can still add comments" ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/discussions/)). Word, Figma and Confluence instead hide resolved comments from the contextual view and keep them behind a filter ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/using-modern-comments-in-word); [Figma Help](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/360041547593-View-and-manage-comments)). The most compact in-place form found is Reviewable's single gutter marker: "There are one or more resolved discussions on this line. Click to reveal" ([Reviewable](https://docs.reviewable.io/files)). Every tool exposes a resolved filter and a reopen action; Ascribe lacking either would be an outlier. No vendor guidance argues for collapse over hide, so Ascribe's choice is defensible on convention alone. The inference is that a heavily reviewed page needs a bulk hide or a Reviewable-style marker, since done inlines cluttering long diffs was a recorded Phabricator complaint ([Phabricator T8909](https://secure.phabricator.com/T8909), snippet).

### Markers that open a panel are the standard narrow-width fallback

Google Docs on mobile has users tap the highlighted text and choose "View comment" ([Google Docs Help](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/65129?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DiOS), snippet), and Notion Minimal is the same pattern as a user setting ([Notion Help](https://www.notion.com/help/comments-mentions-and-reminders)). Ascribe's narrow layout is therefore conventional. That Google and Notion expose the compact mode as a preference suggests, by inference, that some reviewers want it at wide widths too. Documentation here is thin: no source gives the width at which Docs or Word collapse the margin, GitHub listed small-screen support as still in development in June 2025 ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-06-26-improved-pull-request-files-changed-experience-now-in-public-preview/)), and one editor's test of Word's modern comments found them too far from the text without a very wide monitor ([CyberText](https://cybertext.wordpress.com/2021/05/16/word-testing-modern-comments-and-issues-found/), snippet, 2021).

## Three views are universal; both ends of a move and labelled removals are the open questions

### The changes/final/original toggle maps onto every mainstream tool

Word offers All Markup, No Markup ("the document with changes incorporated") and Original, plus Simple Markup, which shows clean text with "a red line in the margin"; "deletions are marked with a strikethrough, and additions are marked with an underline" ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/track-changes-in-word-197ba630-0f5f-4a8e-9a77-3712475e806a)). Google Docs lets reviewers "preview what your document will look like with or without the changes" ([Google Docs Help](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/6033474)). GitBook defaults to a split before/after view with a switch to inline ([GitBook](https://gitbook.com/docs/collaborate/change-requests.md)). Inference: plain-language labels that say which clean view is before and which is after are clearer than Word's "No Markup" and "Original", and Simple Markup is a precedent for a fourth, low-noise state (final text plus margin bars only) that Ascribe could offer cheaply. Practitioner advice to read a clean copy after reviewing tracked changes ([Sophie Michals](https://sophiemichals.medium.com/editing-tip-always-read-a-clean-copy-after-tracking-changes-a79f9fa1b21d), snippet) supports treating the final view as a first-class reading mode.

GitHub's rich diff sets the baseline Ascribe improves on. It marks additions green and removals red, adds "a tooltip describing changes to attributes that, unlike words, would not otherwise be visible", restricts comments to the source view, and can fail on complex documents so that reviewers must fall back to source ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/working-with-files/using-files/working-with-non-code-files)); users report the error "we're unable to render the document prior to diffing" on modified files as "intermittently unreliable" ([community discussion #44477](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/44477)). Rendered-HTML diffing has documented false positives and blind spots: Read the Docs lists "invisible changes" and tables "shown to have changes when they have not actually changed" ([Read the Docs](https://docs.readthedocs.com/platform/stable/visual-diff.html)), and MediaWiki's visual diff misses invisible metadata and struggles with complex tables, while describing non-textual changes "in notes on the side" ([MediaWiki](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/VisualEditor/Diffs)). Inferences: Ascribe needs a defined per-block fallback (show the block as removed plus added, never fail the page); it needs a visible treatment for changes with no rendered footprint such as link targets, image sources and alt text, where side notes beat hover-only tooltips for keyboard and touch users; and deciding block status first and word-diffing only inside changed blocks avoids the fragmented `ins`/`del` output that htmldiff ports admit to ([htmldiff-js](https://github.com/dfoverdx/htmldiff-js), snippet). When most of a paragraph is rewritten, old-then-new blocks read better than interleaved word marks; no source gives a threshold.

### Tools that detect moves mark both ends and link them

Word marks the origin with green double strikethrough and the destination with green double underline ([WordBanter](https://www.wordbanter.com/showthread.php?t=130918), snippet, forum answer). MediaWiki's two-column diff gives moved paragraphs arrows, and clicking an arrow jumps between the two locations ([Wikipedia Help:Diff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Diff), snippet); before 2018 a move "was displayed as if it had been removed and some other text had been added", and the improved diff also highlights changes made inside moved paragraphs ([Wikimedia](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2018/08/24/wikimedia-diff-comparison-improved/)). Wikimedia's design discussion supplies the cautions: a designer found that "the current triangles are not obvious", proposals included move markers with a text excerpt that link to the destination and a simple up/down indicator, and a developer questioned whether "moved content should be shown twice in its entirety" ([Phabricator T169325](https://phabricator.wikimedia.org/T169325)). Git treats a block as moved only when it has "at least 20 alphanumeric characters" ([git-diff](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-diff)).

Inference: if Ascribe marks only the destination, it covers the distinct-treatment and link conventions but not the origin end. The low-noise form that reconciles Word's practice with Wikimedia's worry is a one-line linked stub at the origin ("Moved to ...") and full content with word-level marks at the destination. Move detection needs a minimum size, since short paragraphs, list items and headings produce false moves. No source addresses moves across pages, and no usability study on move presentation was found.

### Collapsed removals have no direct precedent, so label them

Removed text is shown inline and struck through in Word All Markup, Google Docs and GitHub rich diff, or hidden behind a marker in Word Simple Markup. No source gives guidance on how much deleted text to show, and none shows a collapsed-but-expandable removed block; the nearest analogue is GitLab's stepwise expansion of unchanged context ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/merge_requests/changes/)). Inference: collapsing whole removed blocks to an expandable stub is a reasonable middle position that should be tested. Word-level deletions inside a changed block should stay inline, as every tool does. The stub needs a text label ("Removed"), because MDN states that `del` "is not announced by most screen reading technology in its default configuration" ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/del)). Mintlify lists deleted files as non-clickable entries with no diff ([Mintlify](https://www.mintlify.com/docs/editor/review)); a deleted docs page is a change reviewers want to read, so that is a pattern to avoid.

### Navigation needs a count with a stated scope and a next-page step

Word provides Next and Previous plus a Reviewing Pane whose summary "displays the exact number of visible tracked changes and comments that remain" ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/track-changes-in-word-197ba630-0f5f-4a8e-9a77-3712475e806a)). Read the Docs, the closest precedent to Ascribe, puts a changed-file selector at the top right of the rendered page, up/down arrows to jump between chunks and a `d` hotkey to toggle the diff ([Read the Docs](https://docs.readthedocs.com/platform/stable/visual-diff.html)). GitBook's floating navigator lets reviewers "jump between changed blocks, continue to the next page with diffs" and scopes the table of contents to all pages or changed pages only ([GitBook](https://gitbook.com/docs/collaborate/change-requests.md)). Mintlify's preview widget lists added, modified and removed files with search ([Mintlify](https://www.mintlify.com/docs/deploy/preview-deployments)). Inferences: a "change N of M" indicator should state whether M is this page or the whole pull request; Next at the last change on a page needs defined behaviour, and continuing to the next changed page has precedent; a text list of changes doubles as the accessible alternative to visual scanning. No source documents how any tool steps into changes hidden inside tabs, accordions or collapsed blocks. Revealing the container, then scrolling and focusing, with a collapsed removal counted as one stop, is a design decision to test.

## "Pending" is the most misread word in pull-request review

### GitHub's own users ask for "Draft" and "only visible to you"

GitHub's flow is "Start a review", then "Add review comment", then "Review changes" with Comment, Approve or Request changes and "Submit review"; the docs state that "your line comments are pending and only visible to you" ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/collaborating-with-pull-requests/reviewing-changes-in-pull-requests/reviewing-proposed-changes-in-a-pull-request)). The failure is well documented. A discussion titled "Make it clear that 'PENDING' comments have to be submitted" had **423 upvotes**; its author took two months to learn that the badge meant unsent, others report comments unseen for days or months and "outdated by the time they are submitted", and the proposed relabels are "Draft", "Unsubmitted" and "Not yet submitted" with "only visible to you" wording ([community discussion #10369](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/10369)). The VS Code GitHub Pull Requests extension has the same confusion in its tracker ([issue #2822](https://github.com/microsoft/vscode-pull-request-github/issues/2822), title only). Bitbucket's wording is "visible only to you until you submit them" ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/bitbucket/batch-comments), snippet).

Inference: a bar reading "N pending" inherits the complaint. The community's suggested copy is ready-made direction for both the bar and the per-comment badge, and the visibility statement belongs at the reply site too. Replies made during a review join the batch (the reply input's optional pending-review field was seen only in secondary sources: [agynio/gh-pr-review](https://github.com/agynio/gh-pr-review), snippet), so a reviewer who "just replies" silently fails to communicate. GitLab solves this by offering "Add comment now" beside "Start a review" at every comment box ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/merge_requests/reviews/)); Gerrit and Reviewable remove the ambiguity by making drafts the only mode ([Gerrit](https://gerrit-review.googlesource.com/Documentation/user-review-ui.html)). An explicit send-now escape hatch is the inference for Ascribe. A persistent bar helps only while the PR is open in the editor; Astro's `beforeTogglingOff` hook, which cancels closing the toolbar app when it returns a falsy value ([Astro](https://docs.astro.build/en/reference/dev-toolbar-app-reference/)), is the documented place to guard unsent comments in the overlay. No source was found for any tool warning on navigation with unsent drafts.

### Previewing the batch at submit is now table stakes, and Discard belongs inside it

GitHub's January 2026 default Files changed page states: "Pending comments are now shown in the review submission panel, so you can double-check your feedback before submitting" ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-01-22-improved-pull-request-files-changed-page-on-by-default/)). GitLab's "Your review" opens a drawer of all pending comments, editable before submission, and Gerrit's Reply dialog shows the "inline draft comments that will be published" ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/merge_requests/reviews/); [Gerrit](https://gerrit-review.googlesource.com/Documentation/user-review-ui.html)). Inference: a submit dialog showing only a count and a summary field is behind this baseline; it should list each pending comment with a jump link.

GitHub's documented route to abandon a review is to "click Submit review, then click Discard review", which places the destructive action inside the submit surface ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/collaborating-with-pull-requests/reviewing-changes-in-pull-requests/reviewing-proposed-changes-in-a-pull-request)). NN/g's guidance is to "use a confirmation dialog before committing to actions with serious consequences — such as destroying users' work", to "be specific and inform users about the consequence", and to label buttons with outcomes instead of Yes/No, while not confirming routine actions ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/)). Inferences: Discard as a sibling of Submit on a persistent bar is more reachable than upstream makes it and should move into the submit surface or be visually separated; its confirmation should carry the count and outcome-named buttons ("Discard 4 unsent comments?" with "Discard comments" and "Keep review"); submitting needs no extra confirmation. Whether GitHub itself confirms a discard is not stated in the docs read. Server-side deletion makes true undo hard, so a local snapshot offered as "Restore" is the nearest equivalent.

Tools disagree on whether resolving is batched. GitLab lets a pending comment carry a resolve or reopen choice, and Reviewable publishes disposition changes only with the batch ([Reviewable](https://docs.reviewable.io/discussions.html)). No primary source was found for GitHub's behaviour when resolving during a pending review. Inference: because there is no convention, Ascribe has to state its rule in the UI.

### Sync state is where the incumbent extension loses comments

The VS Code GitHub Pull Requests extension prompts to pull when the local branch is out of date, governed by `githubPullRequests.pullBranch` ([extension changelog](https://open-vsx.org/extension/GitHub/vscode-pull-request-github/changes), snippet). Its tracker records the hazards Ascribe shares: an open report of "Lost, overridden or misplaced pending comments" in which contents are "swapped/overridden or moved around in the file", linked by the reporter to delayed or out-of-order sync ([issue #6950](https://github.com/microsoft/vscode-pull-request-github/issues/6950)), and a fix for suggestions "offset by one line if you make local changes first" (snippet, same changelog). GitHub's web redesign had a March 2026 report of pending comments disappearing from view after soft navigation while still posting correctly on submit ([community discussion #189088](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/189088)). Inferences: serialise writes and show per-comment sync state; give "behind" and "ahead" separate notices that each carry their one-click remedy (pull, push), following the incumbent's prompt; and where Ascribe refuses a comment on an unpushed or locally edited line, say why and name the remedy, since GitHub's web UI now accepts comments on any line of a changed file ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-06-26-improved-pull-request-files-changed-experience-now-in-public-preview/), snippet) and users of the incumbent will not expect a refusal. Precedent here is thin: nothing was found on divergence messaging in JetBrains, GitHub Desktop or the CLI, or on signed-out states.

## Overlays on a live page should start small, move, and get out of the way

Vercel's toolbar is the reference for restraint. It starts "sleeping" and "will not run any tools in the background or show comments on pages" until clicked; it is draggable to either side, where it "will snap into place"; it can be hidden by shortcut, disabled for a session, and skipped for automated tests ([Vercel Toolbar](https://vercel.com/docs/vercel-toolbar); [Managing the toolbar](https://vercel.com/docs/vercel-toolbar/managing-toolbar)). Its 2024 changes shrank the toolbar when inactive so it "stays out of the way when you don't" need it and stopped opening the comment editor on text selection ([Vercel Changelog](https://vercel.com/changelog/improvements-to-vercel-toolbar)). Netlify's Drawer sits at the bottom of the window, toggles by keyboard shortcut, and syncs comments both ways with the pull request ([Netlify](https://docs.netlify.com/deploy/review-deploys/netlify-drawer-for-feedback/overview/)). Docs products place change controls at the top right (Read the Docs), in a bottom floating bar (GitBook) or as a bottom-right button (Mintlify). Inference: an overlay that draws marks and threads on page load is more intrusive than the leading precedent, so a one-key toggle for all marks and a remembered collapsed state are the minimum. How Vercel anchors comments to the DOM is undocumented, and no vendor makes an accessibility claim for its overlay.

Astro constrains the overlay half. A toolbar app renders into a ShadowRoot, placement is bottom-left, bottom-centre or bottom-right, and `onToolbarPlacementUpdated` tells apps when it changes ([Astro](https://docs.astro.build/en/reference/dev-toolbar-app-reference/)). A summarised reading of the window source reports `width: min(640px, 100%)`, `max-height: 480px`, 72px above the bottom edge, a hard-coded dark background and a `forced-colors` rule ([window.ts](https://github.com/withastro/astro/blob/main/packages/astro/src/runtime/client/dev-toolbar/ui-library/window.ts)); these values should be re-checked against the file, and the notes record an unresolved conflict over whether it is `fixed` or `absolute`. Astro publishes no design guidance on panel size, occlusion or accessibility for third-party apps. Inferences: at 320px the stock window is full width and covers most of a short viewport, so a multi-field submit form does not belong in it unchanged; NN/g's view that bottom sheets are transient and unsuited to long or complex content ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/bottom-sheet/), a mobile-focused opinion piece) points the same way.

WCAG 2.2 turns occlusion into testable criteria. Under 2.4.11, a focused component must not be "entirely hidden due to author-created content"; sticky footers and non-modal dialogs are the named offenders, `scroll-padding` is a listed remedy, and only the initial position counts for content the user can move ([W3C 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html)). Under 1.4.10, content must work at 320 CSS px without two-dimensional scrolling, and W3C advises that fixed components at small viewports become static or user-toggleable ([W3C 1.4.10](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html)). Under 2.5.8, pointer targets need 24 by 24 CSS px or equivalent spacing ([W3C 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)). Inference: check thread markers, gutter marks and icon-only next/previous buttons against 2.5.8, add bottom scroll padding while the panel is open, and make the panel movable or collapsible.

The VS Code half has different conventions. The guidance is "Only use webviews when absolutely necessary", "Ensure all elements in the view are themeable", and "Use command actions in the toolbar and in the view" ([VS Code webview guidelines](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/ux-guidelines/webviews)). Webviews expose `vscode-light`, `vscode-dark` and `vscode-high-contrast` body classes, `--vscode-*` colour variables, and `vscode-using-screen-reader` and `vscode-reduce-motion` classes ([Webview API](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/extension-guides/webview)); theme colours exist for diff insert and remove backgrounds, gutter added/modified/deleted, and resolved and unresolved comment borders ([Theme Color reference](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/references/theme-color)). Inferences: in VS Code the view toggle, change navigation and refresh belong in native editor title actions, with only marks and threads in the webview; reusing the theme variables gives high-contrast support for free; marks that rely on a translucent fill risk vanishing in high-contrast themes and need a border; scroll animation should respect reduced motion. The VS Code Comments API and the GitHub Pull Requests extension's treatment of outdated and resolved threads were not researched in depth.

## Text labels and a change list are the dependable accessibility channel

WCAG 1.4.1 states that "color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information" ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). Read the Docs' red/green backgrounds and GitHub's rich diff rely on colour as documented; GitHub's answer for code diffs was colour-blind themes that swap "red and green ... for orange and blue" ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2022-04-19-protanopia-deuteranopia-colorblind-themes-beta/), snippet). Inference: every Ascribe change type should survive in greyscale. Underline for word-level insertions and strikethrough for deletions follow Word; a solid versus dashed bar is a valid but subtle cue, so a visible text label on changed, removed and moved blocks as well as added ones is the robust form. With complete non-colour cues, an alternative palette is a comfort feature. In forced-colors mode background fills disappear, so underlines, strikethroughs and borders must carry the meaning; this point and the contrast thresholds of 1.4.3 and 1.4.11 were not verified against a fetched source.

Screen reader support for `ins` and `del` is disputed. MDN says `del` is not announced by most screen readers by default ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/del)); Vispero reported in 2023 that "JAWS and NVDA convey ins, del and mark semantics", that only NVDA supports the ARIA `insertion` and `deletion` roles, and that Narrator conveys none ([Vispero](https://vispero.com/resources/screen-readers-support-for-text-level-html-semantics/)); Adrian Roselli records JAWS adding support in March 2020 and rolling it back that May "in response to customer feedback" ([Roselli](https://adrianroselli.com/2017/12/tweaking-text-level-styles.html)). No test data is newer than September 2023. The hidden-text workaround ("[deletion start]") is cautioned against by both MDN and Roselli for verbosity. Inference: use real `ins` and `del` because they are correct and free, do not depend on them, give each block one label instead of wrapping every word, and treat the clean final and original views as accessibility features because they give two linear readings. The `mark` role prohibits `aria-label` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Accessibility/ARIA/Reference/Roles/mark_role)), so change type cannot be named on the mark itself. The `comment` role with `aria-details` is the intended semantics for threads attached to text, but it is "proposed in WAI-ARIA 1.3 ... still being drafted" ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Accessibility/ARIA/Reference/Roles/comment_role)), and its screen reader support is unknown.

Two interaction criteria apply directly. Under 1.4.13, content shown on hover or focus must be dismissible without moving pointer or focus, hoverable, and persistent ([W3C 1.4.13](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/content-on-hover-or-focus.html)). Astro's own highlights are focusable and show tooltips on focus as well as hover, but the summarised source shows no Escape handling ([highlight.ts](https://github.com/withastro/astro/blob/main/packages/astro/src/runtime/client/dev-toolbar/apps/utils/highlight.ts)). Under 4.1.3, status messages must be programmatically determinable, with `role="status"`, `role="alert"` and `role="log"` as the techniques ([W3C 4.1.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/status-messages.html)). Inferences: thread popovers need Escape, pointer entry and no timeout; reply and resolve controls must be reachable by focus, not hover alone; markers and change stops must be in the tab order; and "change N of M", refresh progress, pending-count changes and submit results are status messages, with errors as alerts. If the view toggle uses tab semantics, the APG pattern expects arrow-key movement and `aria-selected` ([APG Tabs](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/tabs/)); whether a view switch merits tabs is a judgement call the pattern does not settle.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the design review. Ascribe's structural decisions are safe: they are the convention, and where they differ from GitHub they differ in the direction GitHub's own users have asked for. The risk concentrates in states and words. Unsent, approximate, detached, outdated, removed and moved are each a state that some established tool rendered ambiguously and was criticised for, and in each case the fix users requested was the same: say the state in plain text, say why, and offer the way back to the original context. A review that walks each of those six states and asks "what does the reviewer read, and what does a screen reader announce?" will find more than one that inspects layout.

The gaps set the testing agenda. No public evidence settles the connector line, collapsed removals, stepping into hidden content, the word-versus-block threshold, cross-page moves, or current screen reader behaviour for `ins`, `del` and the `comment` role. These are Ascribe's to test with real reviewers and assistive technology, and the several figures here that rest on snippets or summarised source (the Astro window dimensions, the pending-reply API field, Word's move formatting) should be confirmed before the review cites them.
