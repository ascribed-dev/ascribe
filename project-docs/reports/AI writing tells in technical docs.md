# AI tells repeat old style-guide faults

The tools that remove AI writing patterns all draw on one source, Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing", and the better ones have settled on one design: a catalog of patterns weighted by confidence, a deterministic checker for the countable ones, and a model pass for the rest. Three Markdown skill files hold most of the adoption (blader/humanizer at about 64k GitHub stars, hardikpandya/stop-slop at about 18.6k, conorbronsdon/avoid-ai-writing at about 5.4k), and four Vale packages target the tells, of which only tbhb/vale-ai-tells says it is built for technical documentation. Of the roughly thirty patterns the catalogs share, about a dozen do real damage in docs because they change what a reader believes about the software: "serves as" in place of "is", trailing "-ing" benefit clauses, "associated with" in place of a stated relationship, evaluative adjectives in place of specs, unspecific "it's important to note" caveats, cycling synonyms for one thing, and triplets padded to three. The rest are essay and marketing rhetoric, which matters for a product site and a README pitch but seldom for a reference page. Measured evidence is strong for very little: LLM-favored words rose 10 to 35 times in academic corpora, and instruction-tuned models write present-participial clauses at 2 to 5 times the human rate. No study measures any tell in technical documentation, and no study found measures the most-cited tell, "not X but Y". Most word-level tells are already banned by Google, Microsoft, and GitLab for plain-language reasons, so a skill can justify them as quality faults rather than as "sounds like AI". Where the guides endorse a supposed tell (bold term lists, a tutorial's goal sentence, three-item lists, Note callouts, em dashes), the fault is overuse or the wrong place, and page type decides which. Artifacts, fixed phrases, heading case, and punctuation density are mechanical; emptiness, restatement, and whether a list or a label belongs need judgment.

## Three skill files and one Wikipedia page shape every tool

Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" is an advice page from WikiProject AI Cleanup, and almost every skill and rule set cites it or copies from it. It groups signs under Content, Language and grammar, Style, Communication intended for the user, Markup, and Citations, and keeps separate lists of "Ineffective indicators", "Signs of human writing", and, since 2026, "Historical indicators" for tells that newer models have dropped, such as promotional tone, section summaries, em dashes, and elegant variation. It calls itself "descriptive, not prescriptive", says each sign is "only a potential sign of a problem, not the problem itself", and warns that fixing surface features can make the deeper problem harder to find. Its root-cause account is the most useful idea in the whole literature: models "regress to the mean", replacing specific, unusual facts with generic, positive, important-sounding statements ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). It also says it is less useful for writing that isn't informational, and that it is tuned to Wikipedia, so its strongest content signs (notability claims, legacy, "Challenges and future outlook" sections) have only weak counterparts in docs.

The skill files differ more in design than in content. **blader/humanizer** lists 26 patterns in six families (staging instead of stating, rhythm by rule, inflation and borrowed authority, formatting by rule, chat and draft leftovers, writing for the wrong reader), names the five strongest tells that justify an edit on a single sighting, and marks dashes, stacked qualifiers, hyphenated pairs, passive voice, and curly quotes as "weak alone". It drafts, critiques what still sounds artificial, and writes a final version; it asks rather than invent a missing fact; and it keeps "technical and reference prose neutral and plain" while leaving code, data, frontmatter, and link targets untouched ([GitHub](https://github.com/blader/humanizer)). **hardikpandya/stop-slop** is a short set of absolute rules for general prose: banned phrases, structural clichés, no em dashes, no adverbs, active voice only, plus a 1-to-10 rubric on five dimensions that sends text under 35 of 50 back for revision. It says nothing about technical writing ([GitHub](https://github.com/hardikpandya/stop-slop)). **conorbronsdon/avoid-ai-writing** is the most engineered: 74 pattern categories, a 112-entry replacement table split into tiers, a detect mode that separates clear problems from judgment calls, a dependency-free 0-to-100 scorer, a pre-commit hook that fails at 6 findings per file, and a validator that checks code, links, and structure survive a rewrite. Its default context is "technical", it refuses to edit source code, config, or generated data, and it carves out bare noun phrases in changelogs and parameter docs ([GitHub](https://github.com/conorbronsdon/avoid-ai-writing)). A fourth, realrossmanngroup/no_ai_slop_writing_rules, packages 24 rules with WRONG and RIGHT examples ([GitHub](https://github.com/realrossmanngroup/no_ai_slop_writing_rules)). The dozens of other "deslop", "unslop", and "no-slop" skills on directory sites are mostly forks or copies of humanizer and stop-slop.

The linters split into Vale packages and standalone regex scorers. The general-purpose linters (proselint, write-good, alex) have no AI-specific rules.

| Tool | Form | What it adds | Docs-aware |
|---|---|---|---|
| [tbhb/vale-ai-tells](https://github.com/tbhb/vale-ai-tells) | Vale, about 137 rules plus 15 for commit messages | Heading rules (announcement, explainer, marketing), contrast formulas, metacommentary, about 45 figurative-verb rules; notes which rules are noisy | Yes, says it targets technical documentation; every rule defaults to `error` |
| [ammil-industries/vale-signs-of-ai-writing](https://ammil.industries/signs-of-ai-writing-a-vale-ruleset) | Vale, built from the Wikipedia page | Three tiers by confidence: error for definite artifacts, warning for likely patterns, suggestion for what humans also write | General |

| [slop-guard](https://eric-tramel.github.io/blog/2026-02-18-slop-guard) | Python and Rust regex scorer, MCP server | One density score per 1,000 words with ship, review, and revise bands | General |

| [anti-slop](https://marketplace.visualstudio.com/items?itemName=SkeinLLC.anti-slop) | VS Code extension and npm CLI | Flags vocabulary, em dashes, and rule-of-three; never rewrites | Markdown |

Voices makes the clearest case for splitting the work. A skill prompt cost 3,777 tokens per request against nothing for rules on a clean draft, and Vale took about 50 ms against 23.5 s for a model review. It also states the limit plainly: the model caught vague wording no rule could, and fixing violations introduced new ones, so one draft took four passes to go from 25 findings to none ([Vale blog](https://vale.sh/blog/voices)).

### Weighted signals outperform flat bans

The stronger tools treat tells as signals with weights. Humanizer's weak-alone class, avoid-ai-writing's tiering of clarity edits such as "utilize" below frequency markers such as "delve" "so wordiness fixes alone don't push a document toward an AI classification", ammil's confidence tiers, and the domain carve-outs all exist to keep careful human writing from being flagged ([avoid-ai-writing](https://github.com/conorbronsdon/avoid-ai-writing)). The criticisms land on the flat lists. Stop-slop's "kill all adverbs" rule deletes words like "really", "just", and "actually" that sometimes express real uncertainty, its changelog only ever adds rules, and humanizer costs about 2.3 times all of stop-slop's files in context each time it runs ([Capital & Compute](https://capitalandcompute.net/blog/stop-slop-vs-humanizer/)). A journalist's test found humanizer's rewrite still unnatural, claiming to have removed em dashes it hadn't, and GPTZero still rated it 100% AI ([Tech Brew](https://www.techbrew.com/stories/2026/01/21/ai-writing-claude-code-plugin)). Slop-guard's author reports that its first version gave a perfect score to a post a human had flagged for repeated contrast pairs, that the scorer was calibrated on only seven pre-2020 essays, and that a model can learn to dodge specific regexes and produce new formulas ([Tramel](https://eric-tramel.github.io/blog/2026-02-18-slop-guard)). The lesson for a docs skill is that density and repetition matter more than single hits, and that a word list is a snapshot.

No tool checks accuracy against the code. The docs community counts that as the main failure of AI-written docs: practitioners describe factual errors in clean prose and docs that describe the code instead of where users get stuck, while the Write the Docs community's loose consensus is that readers often can't tell AI docs from human ones by phrasing alone ([passo.uno](https://passo.uno/reconsider); [I'd Rather Be Writing](https://idratherbewriting.com/blog/whats-wrong-ai-webinar-recording)).

## About a dozen patterns change what a docs reader believes

The catalogs overlap heavily. After Wikipedia, the main ones are tropes.fyi by Ossama Chaib ([gist](https://gist.github.com/ossa-ma/f3baa9d25154c33095e22272c631f5a1)) and two derivatives, Alex Reynolds' "AI writing: avoid the slop" ([Reynolds](https://www.alexreynolds.com/tools/guides/anti-slop)) and Louis-François Bouchard's "Anti-Slop Writing Guide" ([Bouchard](https://github.com/louisfb01/ai-engineering-cheatsheets/blob/main/Anti_Slop_AI_Writing_Guide.md)). They add essay tics Wikipedia doesn't name: staged reveals, self-answered questions, punchy fragments, pedagogical hand-holding, invented concept labels, and what Reynolds calls hollow fluency, "grammatical sentences that teach the reader nothing". No technical-writing blog or Write the Docs page publishes a catalog of its own.

Sorted by what each pattern does to a reader of technical docs and developer-product copy, the shared list falls into five groups. The harm judgments are inference; no source measured harm to docs readers.

| Pattern | Example | What it does in docs | Listed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hides meaning** | | | |
| Copula avoidance | "The `config` object serves as the central hub" | A definition stops being a definition | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi, Reynolds, humanizer |
| Trailing "-ing" clause | "Caching is per request, ensuring optimal performance" | Claims a consequence without saying how or when | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi, Reynolds, humanizer |
| Vague connection | "the tokens associated with your account" | Withholds the relationship (owned by, scoped to) the reader came for | Wikipedia, as one of the most common current signs |
| Evaluative adjective for a spec | "a robust, seamless integration" | Stands in for what it survives or supports | All catalogs |
| Unspecific caveat | "It's important to note that results may vary" | Reads as a warning, says nothing about what fails | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi, Reynolds, Bouchard |
| Templated or stacked hedging | "may typically" over deterministic behavior | Hides whether the behavior is guaranteed | Reynolds; Wikipedia for the disclaimer form |
| Elegant variation | "workspace… project… environment" for one thing | A new word reads as a new thing | Wikipedia (historical), Belcher |
| Padded triplet in prose | "fast, reliable, and scalable" | Implies exactly three when the real list differs | All catalogs |
| **Slows the reader** | | | |
| Signposting and metacommentary | "In this section, we'll discuss…", "Let's dive in" | Delays the content | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi, Bouchard, tbhb |
| Summary closers | "In summary", "Overall,", recap sections | Repeats what was just read where a next step belongs | Wikipedia (historical), tropes.fyi |
| Heading restated in first sentence | "## Caching" then "Caching is a feature that…" | Spends the first line on nothing | humanizer |
| Pedagogical voice | "Think of it like a highway system for data" | Condescends; an analogy replaces the mechanism | tropes.fyi, Reynolds |
| Hollow fluency | A paragraph with no new fact | The umbrella failure the others are instances of | Reynolds, Bouchard |
| **Breaks scanning** | | | |
| Bold scattered through prose | Every key term bolded | When everything is emphasized nothing is | Wikipedia, Bouchard |
| Bold-label bullets used for reasoning | "**Security**: Environment-based configuration with…" | Breaks an argument into labeled fragments | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi, Reynolds |
| Heading inflation | A heading per paragraph, headings holding only headings | Inflates the table of contents | Wikipedia, Bouchard |
| Title-case headings | "Impact of Technology and Digitalization" | Low harm; inconsistency | Wikipedia, citing Russell et al. |
| Emoji in headings or bullets | "🚀 Getting Started" | Hurts scanning and screen readers | Wikipedia, Bouchard |
| **Breaks things** | | | |
| Curly quotes and Unicode in commands | “--flag” in a code span | Breaks copy and paste | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi |
| Chatbot leftovers and placeholders | "I hope this helps", "INSERT_URL", `utm_source=chatgpt.com` | Ships an artifact | Wikipedia, Bouchard |
| **Mostly voice; matters on a product site** | | | |
| Negative parallelism | "Ascribe isn't just a linter, it's a…" | Knocks down a claim nobody made | All catalogs; tropes.fyi calls it the most identified tell |
| False range | "from prototypes to production to enterprise scale" | Implies a scale that isn't there | Wikipedia (2025 revision), tropes.fyi |
| Promotional puffery and stakes inflation | "unlock a seamless developer experience" | Costs credibility with a technical reader | Wikipedia (historical), Reynolds, tropes.fyi |
| Staged reveals and self-answered questions | "The result? Faster builds." | Rhetoric in place of a statement | tropes.fyi, Reynolds, Bouchard |
| Punchy one-line closers | ", and that matters" | An applause line | tropes.fyi, Reynolds, Bouchard |
| Vague attribution | "developers love", "studies show" | An unsupported claim | Wikipedia, tropes.fyi |
| Em dashes | Several per paragraph | Weak signal; voice | All catalogs, now "historical" on Wikipedia |
| Uniform rhythm | Same-length sentences, blocky paragraphs | Monotony; hard to act on in docs | Economist summary, Reynolds |

The first group deserves most of a skill's attention because those patterns are correctness problems. When "the plugin acts as a bridge" leaves the reader unsure whether the plugin is the bridge, or "ensuring consistency" claims a guarantee the code doesn't make, the page misstates the software. The groups split cleanly by kind of page. Reference and how-to pages mostly show the first four groups. Landing pages, README introductions, and launch posts are where the essay rhetoric of the last group meets the docs patterns: puffery, false ranges, negative parallelism, triplets, and emoji headers cluster there. Wikipedia-only signs (notability claims, "Awards and recognition" sections, invalid ISBNs, edit-summary formulas) and fiction tells (the stock name "Elara Voss", "whispering woods") don't apply to docs at all ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

Several listed tells have legitimate technical uses a checker must leave alone. "Ensure" is often the precise verb ("ensure the port is free"). A contrast is often the point in reference ("pass the path, not the file's contents"). Numeric ranges ("from 1 to 65535") are real. Bold-first definition lists for parameters are a convention, and Reynolds exempts technical documentation for exactly that case. FAQ and troubleshooting headings are questions by design. Example.com links are standard. "Critical section" and "robust estimator" are terms of art, and Bouchard's rule covers them: "Keep a listed word only if it is the precise term" ([Bouchard](https://github.com/louisfb01/ai-engineering-cheatsheets/blob/main/Anti_Slop_AI_Writing_Guide.md)).

The catalogs also contradict each other in ways a skill must settle. Wikipedia lists "in order to" and plain qualifiers ("perhaps", "tends to") as signs of human writing, while Reynolds lists "in order to" and reflexive hedging as Claude tells ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); [Reynolds](https://www.alexreynolds.com/tools/guides/anti-slop)). Both are partly right: a single plain qualifier reads as human, and a templated disclaimer or a stack of qualifiers reads as machine.

## Only words and "-ing" clauses have strong measurements

The evidence comes from academic abstracts, peer reviews, essays, news, and press releases, almost all in English. Nothing has been measured on READMEs, API reference, or docs sites; work on AI-generated docs scores usefulness and similarity (BLEU, ROUGE), not style ([arXiv 2408.00686](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.00686)). Everything said about tells in docs is therefore extrapolation.

| Claimed tell | Strength | What was found |
|---|---|---|
| LLM-favored evaluative words | Strong, population-level only | "delves" 28 times its expected rate in 15 million PubMed abstracts; at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts LLM-processed ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016)); "meticulous" 34.7 times in ICLR reviews ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07183)) |
| Present-participial clauses | Strong, controlled | GPT-4o at 5.3 times the human rate (Cohen's d 1.38); instruction-tuned models at 2 to 5 times ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107)) |
| Nominalizations | Strong, two studies | 1.5 to 2 times in instruction-tuned models ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107)); more in ChatGPT essays ([Herbold et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14276)) |
| Em dashes | Real but model-specific | 0 to about 10 per 1,000 words by model, against a human median near 3.8 ([Freeburg](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27006v1)) |
| Uniform sentence and paragraph length | Moderate, older models | Consistent direction, no magnitude reported ([Desaire et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16352v1); [Muñoz-Ortiz et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.09067)) |
| Rule of three | Weak | Annotators noticed it; no rate measured ([Russell et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)) |
| Hedging overuse | Contradicted | ChatGPT essays had fewer epistemic markers than students' ([Herbold et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14276)) |
| Lexical diversity | Conflicting | Higher in ChatGPT essays, lower in LLM news, depending on the comparison |
| Negative parallelism | Unmeasured | No primary study found |
| Bullets, headings, bold | Unmeasured per feature | Only aggregate markdown counts |

The vocabulary evidence is the heaviest but the least portable. Kobak's method measures excess over a human baseline across a population, so every word on the list is also an ordinary human word, and the authors say it is not a detector for a single text ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016)). The lists also decay. In 1.3 million arXiv abstracts, publicized words such as "delve" and "showcasing" peaked in early 2024 and then fell, while less-noticed ones ("significant", "additionally", "crucial", "comprehensive", "enhance") kept rising ([Geng & Trotta](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09606v2)). Instruction tuning, not the base model, appears to cause the bias: base models sit closer to human rates, and model tests are "consistent with RLHF playing a role" ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107); [Juzek & Ward](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11385)). Each new model therefore brings its own preferred words, and a rule should name a class (evaluative, promotional, or showy-connective words) rather than freeze a list. Wikipedia already dates its vocabulary box by model era, and its newest entry (challenge, dependable, leverage, matters, practical, quietly, steady) rests on a single review site ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

The grammatical tells transfer best. Participial tack-ons and nominalizations appear across essays, abstracts, and news, and both conflict with docs norms that favor imperative mood and concrete verbs. That overlap with long-standing plain-language advice is the strongest reason to adopt them.

The em dash is the tell most relevant to a project whose pages Claude drafts, and the weakest as a universal rule. In one 2026 preprint, Claude Opus 4.6 wrote 9.09 em dashes per 1,000 words unconstrained and 0.19 when told "no markdown"; Claude Haiku 3.5 went from 7.51 to 0.18; GPT-5.4 wrote 1.43 and both Llama models wrote none. Published human essays ranged from 0.33 to 17.12, so a heavy human user sits above every model measured ([Freeburg](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27006v1)). That paper is single-author and its own tables disagree for some models. The Economist's 2026 comparison of four chatbots with newspaper and novel prose reached the same conclusion from another direction: em dashes are "no longer reliable markers of AI text", and only Claude uses them more than human writers ([TheOutpost summary](https://theoutpost.ai/news-story/ai-writing-s-biggest-giveaway-has-changed-the-economist-report-reveals-new-detection-patterns-29372/)). Wikipedia moved em dashes to "Historical indicators" and notes, with a citation-needed tag, that sparse comma-and-period punctuation "has become its own sign" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

### Plain technical prose risks being flagged

Detectors misjudge exactly the writing a docs house style produces. Seven detectors flagged 61% of TOEFL essays by non-native writers as AI on average, and 98% were flagged by at least one; having a model simplify native 8th-grade essays raised their misclassification from 5% to 57%. The mechanism is low perplexity ([Liang et al., Patterns](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.02819)). Controlled vocabulary, short declarative sentences, and consistent terminology all lower perplexity, so technical writers likely face the same risk, though no study tests it. Wikipedia's own list of ineffective indicators warns that mixed casual and formal registers "may indicate the casual writing of a person in a technical field" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

Human judges split by experience. Five frequent LLM users, voting by majority, identified 299 of 300 non-fiction articles correctly, while non-experts were near chance and confident. The experts cited vocabulary in 53.1% of explanations and sentence structure in 35.9% ([Russell et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)). Those two are the tells readers who know the style actually notice.

Nothing shows that removing tells makes text better. Teachers rated ChatGPT essays higher in quality than students' ([Herbold et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14276)), so "sounds AI" and "is worse" are separate judgments, and a study of what people call slop found the judgments track coherence and relevance more than origin ([Shaib et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19163), abstract only). The case for each rule has to rest on what it does to the reader.

## Style guides already ban the words and endorse four "tells"

Most of the vocabulary layer of an AI-tell list is old plain-language advice. Google's word list says to eliminate "easy" and "simple", avoid "just" as filler, use "use" for "leverage" and "utilize", and never write "please note"; it also avoids "will", generally avoids "should", reserves "may" for policy and legal statements, and limits "ensure" and "guarantee" to things that can truly be ensured ([Google word list](https://developers.google.com/style/word-list); [Google excessive claims](https://developers.google.com/style/excessive-claims)). Google's tone page rules out buzzwords, figurative language, placeholder phrases, exclamation marks, "Starting all sentences with the same phrase", and "simply" or "It's easy" in procedures ([Google tone](https://developers.google.com/style/tone)). Microsoft says to "remove words that don't add substance", swap "utilize" and "in order to", and omit adverbs such as "quite", "very", "easily", and "effectively" ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/word-choice/use-simple-words-concise-sentences)). GitLab avoids "easily" and "simply" and bans self-referential writing such as "This page shows…" ([GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/)). Diátaxis asks reference to be "austere", with "neutral description" as its key imperative ([Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/reference/)). A skill can cite these guides for most of its word rules, which ages better than citing a model's habits. The showier AI vocabulary ("delve", "tapestry", "pivotal", "underscore") appears in no guide's word list and falls under the general bans on buzzwords and figurative language.

The guides endorse several things the tell lists flag, and on these a naive skill would fight good doc conventions.

| Construct | Tell lists say | Guides say | Resolution for docs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bold run-in labels | Bold-first bullets are a tell | Microsoft prescribes `**Term**. Definition.` term lists ([Lists](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists)); Google allows run-in headings ([Lists](https://developers.google.com/style/lists)); GitLab keeps bold for UI only | Keep in real term or option lists; flag a label that restates its own sentence, or bold labels on every list |
| "This page explains…" openings | Metacommentary | GitLab bans it; Google's word list assumes "this document"; Diátaxis endorses "In this tutorial we will…" and calls "you will learn" presumptuous ([Tutorials](https://diataxis.fr/tutorials/)) | Allow a goal sentence on tutorials; cut it on reference and how-to |
| Three-item lists | Rule of three | Microsoft allows lists of 2 to 7 items | Flag adjective triplets in running prose, by count; never list length |
| Em dashes | Overuse is a tell | Google allows, unspaced ([Dashes](https://developers.google.com/style/dashes)); Microsoft allows, "don't overuse them" ([Dashes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/punctuation/dashes-hyphens/emes)); GitLab bans | Pick a project rule: a ban or a per-paragraph cap |
| Note callouts | "Note that" is filler | Google defines Note, then warns against too many notices or grouping them ([Notices](https://developers.google.com/style/notices)) | Keep the type; flag stacked notices and "Note that" in body prose |
| Closing praise | Applause closer | Diátaxis allows "a mild note of admiration" at a tutorial's end | Allow one recap sentence on tutorials; flag closers elsewhere |
| "Let's" | Signposting ("Let's dive in") | Google avoids; Microsoft encourages; Diátaxis uses it in tutorials | A house decision |

Every guide that addresses headings calls for sentence case, so the title-case tell and the convention agree ([Google headings](https://developers.google.com/style/headings)). No guide addresses negative parallelism or "-ing" significance clauses directly, and none treats the rule of three as a fault.

The docs teams that publish AI policies care about accuracy, not phrasing. Microsoft Learn tests all AI-generated code, has humans review AI content, and labels every article that contains any ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/principles-for-ai-generated-content)). GitLab tells contributors to check AI output for repetition of the page or a linked topic, invented command syntax, API parameters, or UI names, and a new page for something an existing page covers ([GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/)). A practitioner policy adds that deterministic checks should be extended to catch hallucinated commands and APIs ([passo.uno](https://passo.uno/ai-docs-policy-contributions/)). Those checks rank above any stylistic tell, and a docs toolchain can do them better than a prose linter.

## Regex settles artifacts and words; judgment settles emptiness

Vale's check types map onto the tells cleanly. `existence` finds fixed tokens, `substitution` proposes a preferred form, `occurrence` caps a count per sentence or paragraph, `capitalization` enforces sentence case in headings, `metric` computes formulas over counts including `sentence_length_sd`, `sequence` matches part-of-speech patterns, and `script` runs a small Tengo program per block ([Vale docs](https://docs.vale.sh/checks/existence); [metric](https://docs.vale.sh/checks/metric); [sequence](https://docs.vale.sh/checks/sequence); [script](https://docs.vale.sh/checks/script)). The errata-ai Google and Microsoft packages already encode many of the guide rules, including `Microsoft/Wordiness`, `Microsoft/SentenceLength` (30 words), and `Google/Headings` with a hand-kept proper-noun list ([errata-ai/Microsoft](https://github.com/errata-ai/Microsoft/tree/master/Microsoft); [errata-ai/Google](https://github.com/errata-ai/Google/tree/master/Google)).

| Tier | Tells | Mechanism | Noise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deterministic, error-worthy | Chatbot leftovers, placeholders, `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `oaicite`; curly quotes or Unicode dashes inside code; emoji in headings; title-case headings; spaced em dashes; a project's chosen bans | `existence`, `capitalization`, code-span scope | Near zero; ammil's "definite" tier |
| Deterministic, suggestion | "simply", "just", "easily", "obviously"; "leverage", "utilize", "in order to", "via"; "please note", "it's important to note", "worth noting"; "In summary", "In conclusion"; puffery ("powerful", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "best", "fastest"); self-referential openers | `existence`, `substitution` | Low, with exceptions ("just-in-time", "utilize" for resource quantity) |
| Counted, tune on the corpus | AI vocabulary per page; em dashes per paragraph; hedges per sentence; sentence length; uniform rhythm; repeated sentence starts; share of list items opening with a bold label; stacked or dense notices; adjective triplets in prose; fixed forms of "not just X, but Y" and ", ensuring…" | `occurrence`, `metric`, `sequence`, `script`, or the toolchain's syntax tree | Moderate to high; thresholds unvalidated |
| Judgment | Whether a significance claim, "-ing" clause, or caveat carries a fact; vague attribution's fix; restated paragraphs and repetition across linked pages; whether a list, table, label, or note belongs; elegant variation beyond known pairs; tone for the page type; hollow fluency | Model or human pass; embeddings for repetition | n/a |
| Toolchain, above style | Invented commands, flags, API names; untested examples; a new page duplicating an existing one | Check code spans against the real CLI and schema; compare titles | Low if the surface is declared |

Rules on common words are where noise lives. The current `Google/ExcessiveClaims` rule dropped "never", "always", and "ensure" because they accounted for 125 of 142 hits on a 950-file corpus, almost all legitimate instructions such as "never commit secrets" ([Google/ExcessiveClaims.yml](https://github.com/errata-ai/Google/blob/master/Google/ExcessiveClaims.yml)). A GitLab repository whose Vale job ran with failures allowed had 308 findings nobody acted on; scoping the rules to what they "can honestly judge" cut that to 17, of which 107 had come from one misapplied link rule, and the author's verdict was that "a linter whose output is permanently ignored is not help, it is noise with a CI cost" ([GitLab MR !54](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-com/public-sector/reference/-/merge_requests/54)). tbhb flags its own `BareHolds` as "the loudest rule on ordinary technical writing" and warns that `ContrastiveNegation` "may fire on terse spec lists" ([tbhb](https://github.com/tbhb/vale-ai-tells)). Run any rule over the project's own pages and read the hits before promoting it past a suggestion.

Two categories of tell sit badly with Vale and well with a typed toolchain. Position-dependent checks (an opener in the first paragraph, a closer in the last, a heading restated in the first sentence) need to know where a block sits, and Vale's scope for "first paragraph of a document" wasn't confirmed. Page-type-dependent checks (a goal sentence is right on a tutorial and wrong on reference; bold term lists are right on an options page) need the page's type, which Ascribe's content model already declares. Rhythm measures are the weakest mechanical tells for docs specifically: procedures are uniform by design, and consistent terminology lowers lexical diversity on purpose, so uniform rhythm and low diversity are virtues on a task page.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing a docs skill should use. "Avoid sounding like AI" is a poor rule for Ascribe: the evidence is population-level and drawn from essays and abstracts, the word lists go stale within a year, and the house style of plain words and short declarative sentences is the low-perplexity prose detectors already misjudge. The defensible rule is "say what a thing does", which the house style already states, applied to the specific ways models avoid saying it: "serves as" for "is", ", ensuring…" for a stated mechanism, "associated with" for a stated relation, "robust" for a spec, "it's important to note" for a stated failure. Each of those has a plain-language justification in Google, Microsoft, GitLab, or Diátaxis that will hold after the AI association fades, and each is a correctness fault in docs rather than a matter of taste. The essay rhetoric (negative parallelism, staged reveals, false ranges, puffery) belongs in the skill's section for the site and README copy, where it costs credibility, not in its rules for reference pages.

Two findings bear directly on a project whose pages Claude drafts. Claude is the model family that still overuses em dashes, and in one study a single "no markdown" instruction cut Claude Opus 4.6 from 9.09 em dashes per 1,000 words to 0.19 ([Freeburg](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27006v1)), so the cheapest intervention is a project rule (a ban or a per-paragraph cap) stated in the skill and enforced by a check. And over-correction is its own tell: Wikipedia already notes that sparse, comma-and-period prose has started to look machine-made, and its "signs of human writing" include plain "is" and "has", plain verbs, and a well-placed qualifier. A skill that bans every adverb, hedge, and contrast will strip conditions that docs need ("usually", "unless", "not the file's contents") and produce a new uniform style. The skill should therefore keep its deterministic rules few and trusted, put counted rules at suggestion level until they are tuned on Ascribe's own pages, condition placement rules on the page type the content model supplies, and rank checks that a command, flag, or directive actually exists above every stylistic tell.
